Chapter I

The Fellowship

“Not all those who wander are lost.” — J.R.R. Tolkien

Six years on the operations side of software taught me what happens after the merge. At KOBIL GmbH — a cybersecurity and identity stronghold — I managed application versions, wrote the technical documentation, and carried customer integrations, working shoulder to shoulder with the engineering team. Before that came planning, IT consulting, and R&D on industrial sensor systems.

That history shapes how I write code now. I care about deployability, clear API contracts, and what becomes of a service once it ships — not only whether the tests are green.

What I bring is less a finished toolbox than an appetite for the next unfamiliar thing. It is why I went looking for language models before any curriculum reached them, and why the parts of a project I enjoy most are the ones I have not done before.

Today I build across the whole stack: ASP.NET Core and Node.js services at one end, React and Next.js interfaces at the other, with my centre of gravity on the backend. AI-assisted development is part of that workflow rather than an experiment — Claude Code in the terminal, locally hosted models where the data has to stay on the machine. Knowing when the tool genuinely helps, and when it is producing plausible code I would have to unpick later, is the part that takes judgement.

The Master Work

FHIR Encounter Summary

TypeScript · 2026

Takes the scattered FHIR resources of a single hospital encounter, folds them into one ordered domain model, generates a German Epikrise from it with a locally hosted language model — and then marks every number in that generated paragraph as backed or unbacked by the source data.

The engineering is the normalising, not the model: one encounter arrives as 35 separate Observation resources, with dates hiding under four different field names. The grounding check is what makes the output reviewable rather than merely plausible — it caught an age the model had invented outright.

Synthetic data only — every record comes from Synthea. Inference runs locally, so nothing leaves the machine.
